# Claims and Boundaries

ART is deliberately separated into claim strengths.

## Tier A — descriptive observations

These are weak claims intended mainly to define the problem space:

- autonomous actors can cause consequential changes in external systems;
- cross-trust action creates questions of goals, authority, evidence, and consequences;
- state-change descriptions are useful in many domains.

These observations are not presented as original discoveries.

## Tier B — structural hypotheses

Current ART hypotheses include:

1. **Target inevitability hypothesis** — purposeful cross-trust action cannot remain operationally well-defined without reconstructing an equivalent of target/completion semantics.
2. **Bounded-authority hypothesis** — use of another actor's resources or powers requires scope-constrained authority rather than an unbounded boolean permission.
3. **Verification inevitability hypothesis** — across trust boundaries, claimed completion requires an evidence-based determination or a semantic equivalent.
4. **Judgment-separation hypothesis** — in consequential discretionary choice, commitment to a proposition is not fully reducible to intelligence, prediction, authorization, or execution.
5. **Delegation hypothesis** — propagation of authority across actors reconstructs delegation semantics even if a system avoids that term.
6. **Termination-semantics hypothesis** — lifecycle-bound authority or commitment relationships reconstruct expiry/revocation/completion/supersession semantics.
7. **Causality-responsibility separation** — causal contribution and normative responsibility are distinct relations.

## Tier C — strong claims not yet earned

The following claims are intentionally **not** made at v0.1:

- ART is universal;
- ART is unique;
- ART is a natural law;
- Target / Bounded Authority / Verification are a complete primitive set;
- J/D/T/V are the minimal sufficient event primitives;
- every autonomous action requires an explicit Judgment;
- every termination must be represented as a first-class event;
- TSTO is the canonical or inevitable encoding of Target transitions;
- JEP is the canonical or inevitable encoding of judgment-related events.

## Determinability boundary

Technical systems can establish protocol-level facts such as signatures, hashes, references, timestamps, policy evaluations, observed events, and evidence relationships. They cannot automatically prove:

- external-world truth in all domains;
- legal authorization validity;
- moral responsibility;
- legal liability;
- complete causality;
- fairness or legitimacy.

ART studies how these layers relate without collapsing them.

## Originality boundary

ART does not claim invention of state transitions, authorization, delegation, verification, accountability, or related long-standing concepts.

Potential contribution lies in:

- identifying which structures become non-removable under autonomous cross-trust action;
- separating concepts that are often conflated;
- testing whether different engineering traditions converge on a smaller common structure;
- producing falsifiable predictions from that structure;
- providing a layered relationship among theory, target-transition objects, event grammars, and downstream institutional mechanisms.

## Revision discipline

A term should be removed, merged, or demoted if a simpler model explains the same cases with equal or better predictive and engineering performance.
